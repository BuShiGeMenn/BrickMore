1 Introduction
Spatial reasoning and geometric understanding are funda-
mental to human cognition and to artificial intelligence,
supporting applications from robotics and navigation to
computer-aided design. As large language models (LLMs)
have matured, a growing body of work asks whether they
canreasonaboutshape,position,andtransformation.Recent
benchmarksprobethisabilitythroughpaperfoldingandun-
folding(Zhouetal.2026)andthroughgeometryexpressedas
procedural drawing code (Luo et al. 2026), and both report
pronounced deficiencies in current models. These studies
largely assess spatial reasoning by asking a model to select
or compute an answer. A complementary and less examined
question is whether a language model canconstruct: given
onlywords,canitproduceathree-dimensionalobjectwhose
parts are correctly sized, correctly placed, and physically
joined?
LEGO brick assembly makes this question concrete. A
concise request such asa simple chairidentifies an object
categorybutleavesitsdimensions,proportions,andpartlay-
out largely unspecified. Answering it requires three coupled
forms of geometric spatial reasoning. The model must com-
plete the missing geometry from prior knowledge of what
the object looks like. It must discretize that geometry into
pieces of fixed dimensions at integer positions on a grid.
Anditmustrespectthedifferencebetweenspatialadjacency
and stud connectivity: pieces that touch side by side do not
hold together unless they interlock vertically through studs.
We call this setting theText-to-Bricktask: translating
a natural-language description, at any level of detail, into a
setofLEGObrickplacementsthatreproducesthedescribed
shape as a single connected assembly. Because every output
can be checked for coverage, bounds, and connectivity, the
task offers a verifiable test of whether linguistic knowledge
has become spatial structure.
Prior work has shown that language models can be
trained for this task. BrickGPT (Pun et al. 2025) learns au-
toregressive brick-placement sequences and applies validity
checks with physics-aware rollback during inference. Brick-
Net (Kulits and Schmid 2026) adopts a graph-based repre-
sentation that supports a broader range of parts and connec-
tion types. Two questions remain open.(Q1)How well do
generators translatelimitedlinguistic information into ob-
ject geometry? Pooled scores hide this: only a level-wise
evaluation(Table4)shows,forexample,thatBrickNettrails
BrickForma on mean VQAScore, PE, and SigLIP2 even at
the two shortest description levels, L1 and L2.(Q2)Can
LLM-assisted programs encode the geometric details omit-
ted from concise prompts in a form suitable for training a
compact model?
We present BrickForma, which uses executable programs
to generate training examples for a compact text-to-brick
model. With the assistance of GPT-6.1 Sol, we develop
parametric programs that vary object proportions, part di-
mensions,openings,andsupportarrangementstosynthesize
voxelshapesinbatches.Eachprogramspecifiesthestructure
ofanobjectfamilyandexposesitsgeometricdegreesoffree-
dom as parameters. The source collection contains 80,000
models across 21 categories on a483 grid. Each model is
paired with five levels of description that progressively add
structural,geometric,proportional,andappearanceinforma-
tion. The resulting approximately 400,000 caption records
link concise requests and detailed specifications to shared
geometry, providing supervision for prompts with different
amounts of geometric detail.

We fine-tune Qwen3-1.7B (Yang et al. 2025) with low-rank

language model decides what shape a description denotes,
and the solver decides how that shape holds together.

and BrickNet (18.42%). Reporting all five levels links the
